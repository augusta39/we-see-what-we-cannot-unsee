# We See What We Cannot Unsee

Nov 21, 2026 · @Roddsi

Here is my weekend musing: a life is a handful of pursuits sharing one finite clock, and what we have already lived changes what each new moment comes to mean, and so where our time goes next. In short, we see what we cannot unsee.

What follows lays the idea out, draws it as a shape, and checks it against what real data can say so far.

## Why I started

> This essay began over a Friday night (Nov 20, 2026) and continued into the following afternoon.

Lately I have been learning piano and sketching, I am about to start acrylic painting, and I am trying to socialize as much possible. All the while I am pushing through work, because for me, work is life.

But in all of this, I am looking for meaning. Part of it, too, is my obsession with getting to “the heart of the heart,” which Grothendieck *suffered* from (refer to Benjamín Labatut’s *When We Cease to Understand the World*) in his quest to get to the hidden core beneath all of mathematics.

In the language of this essay, the above-mentioned activities are my *pursuits*, each competing for the same finite hours, and what follows is my attempt to get to the heart of why some of those hours come to matter more than others.

## I. Many pursuits, one path

Most of us are pursuing several things at once: work, a craft, friendships, a body that needs keeping, a few questions we cannot leave alone. None of them gets all of us. Each day's hours are divided among them, and the division keeps shifting. A pursuit that once filled half a week can shrink to an evening a month.

**H1.** A finite life holds several pursuits at once, and their priority changes over time.

The constraint is simple and unforgiving. Time given to one pursuit is time withheld from another, so attention is less of a resource we spend and more of a budget we keep re-cutting.

> William James put the stakes plainly in 1890: "My experience is what I agree to attend to." Whatever we stop attending to slowly stops being part of our lives.

**H5.** A life can be drawn as one continuous, nonlinear path through these shifting priorities that bends toward one pursuit and then another.

The path is one line because only one person lives it. It bends because priorities move.

## II. Duration is not significance

We measure experiences in hours, years and outputs: the length of a trip, the years in a job, the papers published, the money earned. These are the *tangible* traces of an experience. They are real, and they are often how we judge what mattered. However, they are a poor guide to what lasts.

**H2.** How long an experience lasted, and what it tangibly produced, do not determine how significant it becomes.

![Duration against later significance · four kinds of experience](figures/duration-vs-significance.png)

*Duration against later significance · four kinds of experience*

If significance were proportional to duration, every experience would sit on the diagonal. The shaded corners are the ordinary exceptions.

Bergson distinguished clock time, which divides into equal units, from lived duration, which does not. The same hour can be empty or full. H2 carries the point from how time feels to what it leaves behind. A brief experience can carry great weight forward; a long one can leave almost nothing.

> The claim is narrower than it sounds. It does not say duration never matters; practice, presence and repetition plainly build significance in many lives. It says duration is not a law. Knowing how long something lasted, or what it produced, does not tell you how much it will matter. Whether, and how much, duration matters in real lives is an open empirical question.

## III. Two clocks: feeling and meaning

An experience runs on at least two clocks. One is feeling: the joy, fear or relief we have while it happens. The other is meaning: how significant the experience becomes, how much it shapes who we take ourselves to be. We tend to assume the two move together. Often they do not.

**H3.** Significance can persist, fade, grow or be reinterpreted after the experience itself has ended.

**H4.** How an experience feels at the time is distinct from how significant it becomes.

![Computed from the two-stage model (appendix A2) · illustrative, not data](figures/two-clocks.png)

*Computed from the two-stage model (appendix A2) · illustrative, not data*

The chart shows the simplest way the clocks can come apart. Feeling spikes during the event and fades within days. Significance builds more slowly, peaks after the event is over, and lingers.

> Refer to the two-stage model in the appendix.

What the curves make clear is that nothing is paradoxical about an experience mattering most long after it stopped hurting or delighting.

Kierkegaard observed that life is lived forwards but understood backwards. H3 adds that the understanding never finishes. A later event can make an old one newly important, or quietly drain its importance away. The past is closed as a sequence of events; as a set of meanings, it stays open to revision.

## IV. We see what we cannot unsee

Everything so far treats experiences one at a time. The central hypothesis is about what happens between them.

**H6.** **Earlier experiences change how later experiences are perceived, interpreted and weighted, and so change which pursuits receive attention.**

Once you have been betrayed, a friend's small lie reads differently. Once you have watched someone recover from an illness, a diagnosis sounds different.

> There is a second part to my original hunch: the tangible and the intangible are not realized together. I love my parents and video call them, but physically we are apart. In the picture of a life later in this essay, that love is a large dot, while its tangible side, shared space and physical presence, stays small. A few minutes on a screen can still land as closeness, because they land on everything that came before them.

The *hermeneutic* *tradition* is where Gadamer argued that understanding always starts from a horizon of prior judgments; there is no view from nowhere. H6 is a narrower and more testable version of that idea. It says the effect of a new experience depends on two things about the old ones: how similar they are to it, and how much they came to matter.

![The H6 loop · four stages, one added step](figures/h6-loop.png)

*The H6 loop · four stages, one added step*

The loop is the whole hypothesis. An experience happens and acquires significance over time. That significance sets how strongly the next similar experience registers. Across many experiences, the accumulated weight shifts which pursuits feel worth our hours, and attention follows. Then the next experience arrives inside a life already tilted by the last ones.

If this is right, two people can live through the same event and leave with different significance, not because one is more sensitive but because each brings a different past to it. It would also explain why some turns in a life feel sudden: a small experience lands on a large, similar past and tips the balance.

None of this is new as an intuition and I don't claim it. Psychology has long shown that prior experience colors how new events are read, and philosophers said it first. What may be new is narrower: **that *changes* in how significant past experiences are predict changes in how later events are interpreted, and in how finite attention is redistributed across competing pursuits, beyond what existing goals, emotional state and prior behavior already explain.** That version can fail in two places. Past significance might shape interpretation but not where the hours go, or it might add nothing beyond existing models.

## V. The whole picture: a life as a pyramid

The hypotheses fit in one picture, which began as a sketch on paper. Three axes carry the measurable side of a life, and all three start from the person, me, at the origin.

- The x axis is the lifespan, t = 0, 1, … up to its end, L.

- The y axis is the time spent so far in each pursuit, P1, P2 and onward, stacked one on another: each band thickens whenever its pursuit gets time, and how steeply it thickens at any moment is that pursuit's share of attention.

- The z axis is the tangible: matter, in space and time, physical and temporal, as the brain registers it. Through this space runs one non-linear path that hops between pursuits, comes back, and crosses several of them.

Notice what is missing from the axes: significance. Lifespan, time spent and tangible matter are the things we can measure, and none of them is meaning. In the picture, significance is not a direction at all; I have depicted it as the size of the dots.

![Conceptual diagram · illustrative shares and events, not data](figures/life-pyramid.png)

*Conceptual diagram · illustrative shares and events, not data*

The shape is a pyramid, and most of its form is not a choice. However the hours are divided, the time spent across all pursuits adds up to the time lived (appendix A1), so the stacked bands always reach exactly your age and their top edge is a straight diagonal. Slice the shape at any age t and the slice is the time spent so far by what has been made so far; at t = 0 both are zero, so the apex is the person at the origin. If a life had no end, the shape would keep opening: the infinite pyramid of the original sketch. A finite life closes it with an end face at x = L, and from that face, looking back, the same experiences can be seen differently without being merged into one.

Every quantity in the model has a place in the picture, including the ones too fast or too diffuse to draw:

And the picture is a projection: a life's full state has more dimensions than any drawing, so the pyramid is a *map*, not the territory.

## VI. What would prove me wrong

An idea this intuitive is easy to believe and hard to test, so it is worth saying exactly what would count against it.

**The prediction.** If H6 is true, a person's earlier appraisals of one kind of experience should predict how they appraise new experiences of the same kind better than their earlier appraisals of other kinds do. If same-kind and other-kind pasts predict equally well, the apparent carryover is just stable temperament, and H6 is wrong in this form.

**The second prediction** tests the part that would be new: changes in an earlier experience's significance should predict later shifts in time spent across pursuits, beyond a person's existing goals, mood and past time use. Daily-diary studies can test the first prediction. The second needs repeated significance ratings and time-use records from the same people over time, which none of the datasets examined so far provides.

Where the evidence stands, from reanalyses of three public datasets:

- **Significance shifts after the fact (H3).** In Hippocorpus, 1,282 people rated how important a memory was (1,310 memories), then rated it again when they retold it weeks to months later; 37% of the ratings moved. Some of that is rating noise (the two ratings correlate 0.62), and many started at the scale's maximum, so this is evidence of revision, but modest evidence.

- **Reinterpretation continues for years (H3).** In MainLife, 123 people narrated their most important life events twice, about four years apart. Events that were older at the first telling changed meaning less over the next four years, by roughly 0.13 to 0.17 points on the study's change scale per decade of age, depending on the model.

- **Feeling and meaning are related but distinct (H4).** In an experience-sampling study of 351 people, positive feeling at one prompt predicted a greater sense of meaning at the next (+0.14 on 1 to 5 scales), and meaning predicted later positive feeling (+0.05). They move together without being the same thing.

- **Significance and attention feed each other (H1).** In a three-month diary of 247 people who had set a personal goal aside, a goal that mattered more than usual drew more thought at the next entry (+0.07 points per point, 95% CI 0.03 to 0.10), and more thought made it matter more at the next entry (+0.09, CI 0.06 to 0.11). A competing priority goal did not detectably crowd that thought out (−0.03, CI −0.08 to 0.01). This is the closest existing evidence for the attention half of the refined claim, though with goals rather than past experiences, and thoughts rather than hours.

- **Tangible accomplishment barely builds meaning (the z axis).** In the same experience-sampling study, getting things done went with a little more meaning in the moment (+0.11), but predicted almost none a few hours later (+0.02, CI −0.00 to 0.04), while positive feeling predicted six times more (+0.13). What gets done and what it comes to mean are different quantities.

- **Memories settle; people don't (the x axis).** In MainLife, an experience a decade older changed its meaning less over the next four years (−0.17, CI −0.28 to −0.05), but older people did not revise less than younger ones (+0.03, CI −0.08 to 0.14). More open people revised more (+0.04 per point on the openness scale). These replicate the published model.

- **The way back fades (the path that comes back).** In the goal diary, most people still meant to return to the goal they had shelved (74% averaged above the midpoint), but over three months that intention fell about 11 points on a 100-point scale, more than the goal's value fell (about 5).

- **Duration against significance (H2)** has not been measured in any dataset analyzed so far.

- **The central claim (H6) is untested.** One attempt paired people's own memories with their judgments of an imagined new event. It returned a null result, but its similarity measure failed a validity check, so it could not decide either way. A decisive test needs many appraised experiences per person; daily-diary studies that follow the same people for years provide that, and a dated protocol for such a test is written.

So the honest status is this: the supporting pieces have evidence, the frame is coherent, and the central claim is a hypothesis with a clear way to fail.

## VII. Why it matters

If significance followed duration, the best advice about living would be arithmetic: give the most hours to what matters most. If H6 is right, the advice is stranger. The experiences we choose do not only fill time; they retune the instrument we use to judge every later experience. A single afternoon can change what a decade feels like, and a decade can fail to change anything.

That is a reason for humility about our own priorities. What feels important now is partly an echo of what happened to matter before. It is also a reason for some freedom. If the lens is built from experience, new experience can rebuild it.

We see what we cannot unsee.

## Appendix: the formal model and proofs

These results are properties of a deliberately simple model. They show the ideas are coherent and say what would have to be measured. They are not empirical findings.

**Definitions.** Time runs over \[0, L\]. Pursuit i has attention share a_i(t) ≥ 0 with Σ_i a_i(t) = 1, cumulative investment τ_i(t) = ∫₀ᵗ a_i(s) ds, latent significance S_i(t), and cumulative tangible achievement F_i(t). Experience j occurs over \[s_j, s_j + d_j\] with exposure x_j(t), feeling E_j(t), and significance C_j(t) with baseline b_j.

**A1. Time accounting (H1).** Cumulative investment across pursuits equals elapsed time.

```math
\sum_i \tau_i(t) = \int_0^t \sum_i a_i(s)\, ds = \int_0^t 1\, ds = t
```

Proof: exchange the finite sum and the integral. If only part of the day is allocable, replace 1 by ρ(t) ∈ \[0, 1\] and the sum equals ∫₀ᵗ ρ(s) ds.

**A2. Two clocks (H3, H4).** Feeling responds fast; significance responds to feeling, more slowly.

```math
\frac{dE_j}{dt} = -\lambda E_j + \alpha\, x_j(t), \qquad \frac{dC_j}{dt} = -\mu\,(C_j - b_j) + \beta E_j
```

For a brief experience of total size U, with C_j(0) = b_j and positive λ ≠ μ:

```math
C_j(t) - b_j = \frac{\beta \alpha U}{\lambda - \mu}\left(e^{-\mu t} - e^{-\lambda t}\right)
```

Proof: E_j(t) = αU e^(−λt); solve the linear equation for C_j − b_j, starting at 0, with an integrating factor. If βαU \> 0, the bracket and the denominator always share a sign, so significance stays above baseline and peaks after the experience has ended, at:

```math
t^{*} = \frac{\ln(\lambda/\mu)}{\lambda - \mu} > 0
```

Proof: the derivative vanishes where λ e^(−λt) = μ e^(−μt); ln(λ/μ) has the sign of λ − μ, so t\* is positive. This delay holds for any two-stage process, so it shows that delayed significance is possible, not that it is common. A third model, in which significance responds to the experience directly rather than through feeling, is equally coherent; telling them apart needs independent measures of feeling.

**A3. Duration does not fix significance (H2).** The model implies no law C_j(t) = k · d_j. Proof by counterexample: two experiences with equal duration but different α or β have different trajectories (A2 scales with βα). Conversely, two brief experiences with the same total size U but different durations, both much shorter than 1/λ, produce nearly the same later significance. This shows proportionality is not implied; it does not show duration has no effect.

**A4. Only relative significance moves attention.** If attention follows a softmax,

```math
a_i = \frac{e^{\theta S_i + h_i}}{\sum_k e^{\theta S_k + h_k}}, \qquad \delta a_i \approx \theta\, a_i \Big(\delta S_i - \sum_k a_k\, \delta S_k\Big)
```

where h_i captures obligations and access. The approximation follows from ∂a_i/∂S_k = θ a_i (1\[i = k\] − a_k). So an experience that raises every pursuit's significance equally moves no attention; only shifts in relative significance, or in constraints, reallocate time. This is a property of the softmax, which is one candidate rule among several.

**A5. Significance and attention can diverge.** Normalize nonnegative significance into shares p_i = S_i⁺ / Σ_k S_k⁺ and define the mismatch G = ½ Σ_i \|p_i − a_i\|. Then 0 ≤ G ≤ 1 and

```math
G = 1 - \sum_i \min(p_i, a_i)
```

Proof: \|p_i − a_i\| = p_i + a_i − 2 min(p_i, a_i), and both shares sum to 1. G is the total-variation distance: 0 means attention matches significance exactly, 1 means they do not overlap at all.

**A6. The H6 term.** Without it, experiences shape pursuits but not each other. H6 lets the strength with which a new experience k registers depend on earlier, similar experiences and how significant they still are when k begins:

```math
\alpha_k = \alpha_0 + \gamma \sum_{j<k} \mathrm{sim}(j,k)\, C_j(s_k)
```

γ = 0 is the null, γ \> 0 is carryover, γ \< 0 is habituation. Consequence: two people given the same experience k follow different significance trajectories whenever their histories differ.

**A7. What can be identified.** γ cannot be separated from stable temperament unless the same person's same-kind and other-kind pasts are compared, which is the prediction in section VI. And no single curve of significance reveals whether feeling carried the effect; that needs feeling measured on its own.

**A8. The pyramid is a projection.** The three drawn axes are the first three coordinates of the life state Γ(t) = (t, τ(t), F(t), S(t), a(t)): lifespan, time spent so far in each pursuit, and tangible achievement. Stacking the τ_i(t) gives a height of Σ_i τ_i(t) = t by A1, so the top edge is the line y = x; if only part of each day is allocable, the height is ∫₀ᵗ ρ(s) ds ≤ t. Each band's slope is dτ_i/dt = a_i(t), so the attention shares appear as slopes, and significance appears as dot size. Depth F(t) starts at 0 and never falls; if it grows in proportion to t, every slice is the same rectangle scaled by t and the solid is a pyramid with its apex at the origin, and otherwise one face curves. With no end of life the solid is unbounded: the infinite pyramid.
